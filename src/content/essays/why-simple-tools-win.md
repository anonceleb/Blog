Most tools that stay useful for a decade have one thing in common: they stayed simple while everything around them got more complicated.

## The hidden tax

Every feature added to a piece of software creates:

- More surface area for bugs
- More concepts users must hold in their head
- More decisions for the maintainers
- More ways the thing can break when the world changes

This tax is invisible on launch day. It shows up three years later when the original authors have left and nobody quite understands why the export button sometimes corrupts files.

## What "simple" actually means

Simple does not mean minimal UI or few lines of code. It means:

- The mental model fits in one screen
- The failure modes are obvious
- You can explain the core behavior to a smart colleague in under two minutes
- Adding one new feature does not require understanding seven other features

Markdown is simple. A plain text file is simple. A single-purpose CLI that does one thing well is simple.

Notion is not simple. Figma is not simple. Modern web frameworks are not simple. They are powerful, which is different.

## The long game

The best tools are the ones you can still use after the company that made them has pivoted, been acquired, or gone out of business. Plain text, open formats, and small programs that read and write them survive.

Everything else is a bet on continued stewardship.
