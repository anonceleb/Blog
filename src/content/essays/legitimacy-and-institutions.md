Institutions run on legitimacy more than they run on rules or money.

When a court, a university, a newsroom, or a government loses legitimacy, the formal powers it still holds start to feel increasingly hollow. People begin to route around it. Compliance becomes performative rather than genuine.

## How legitimacy is created

It is usually built slowly through:

- Consistent behavior over long periods
- Visible competence at the thing the institution claims to do
- Some degree of fairness in how it treats different groups
- A story that the relevant population finds at least partially believable

It can be destroyed much faster.

## Crypto's legitimacy problem

Many early crypto projects understood this instinctively. Bitcoin's legitimacy came from running for years without major failure while its early advocates kept a relatively coherent story about sound money and censorship resistance.

Later projects sometimes tried to buy legitimacy with marketing budgets or celebrity endorsements. It rarely sticks. Legitimacy that can be purchased is not the valuable kind.

## What this means in practice

If you are trying to build something that is meant to last more than a few years, the boring work of being consistent, competent, and fair usually matters more than the exciting work of growth hacks and narrative pushes.

The latter can accelerate attention. Only the former compounds into something people will actually defend when it is attacked.
